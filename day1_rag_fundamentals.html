Module 1 · The problem

Why Do We Need RAG?

⏱ 15 min

What is a Large Language Model?

A Large Language Model (LLM) is a neural network — a Transformer, the architecture you met in Deep Learning — trained on very large amounts of text to predict the next token. Repeating that prediction produces fluent answers, summaries, code and explanations.

🧠 What an LLM uses to answer

1. Parametric knowledge — patterns stored in its weights during training.

2. The prompt (context window) — everything sent in the current request: instructions, conversation, and any documents you paste in.

Nothing else. It does not browse your file share or database unless your application fetches that data and puts it into the prompt.

⚠️ Limits of pretrained knowledge

Knowledge cut-off: it knows nothing after its training data ends.

No private data: your company's policies, contracts, tickets and wikis were never in its training set.

No sources: knowledge in weights cannot be cited or traced to a document.

Expensive to update: changing weights means retraining or fine-tuning.

How hallucinations happen

An LLM is optimised to produce plausible text, not to check facts. When the needed fact is missing, the most plausible continuation may still be a confident, specific — and wrong — answer. Ask about "our annual leave allowance" and a model may answer "20 days" because that number is common in text it has seen, not because it knows your policy.

Why not paste every document into every prompt?

📏 Context limits

Context windows are large but finite. Thousands of policy pages, tickets and manuals will not fit.

💸 Cost & latency

You pay for and wait on every input token, for every question — even when 99% of the text is irrelevant.

🎯 Focus

Burying one relevant sentence among thousands of irrelevant ones can make it harder for the model to use the right evidence.

💡
The RAG idea

First retrieve the few passages relevant to the question, then augment the prompt with them, then let the LLM generate an answer grounded in that evidence.

RAG vs. Fine-Tuning vs. Prompt Engineering

⏱ 10 min

These three techniques solve different problems and are often combined. A useful rule: RAG changes what the model knows for this request; fine-tuning changes how the model behaves; prompting tells the model what to do now.

What exactly is in a vector store?

Each entry holds a vector (the searchable numeric representation) and usually the chunk text (or a reference to where it lives) plus metadata such as source file, page, department and last-updated date. A vector is not a readable copy of the text — you cannot simply read the original sentence back out of the numbers. That is why stores keep the text or a pointer to it: after the search finds the nearest vectors, the application needs the actual words to send to the LLM.

5.1 Documents and text extraction

    
Embedding models and LLMs work on text. Every PDF, Word file, HTML page or Markdown note must first be converted into clean, machine-processable text. This is unglamorous work — and it often decides retrieval quality.

    

      
📕 PDF
Designed for printing, not reading by machines. Watch for headers/footers on every page, multi-column layouts, tables, hyphenated line breaks and scanned pages that need OCR.

      
📝 TXT
Easiest: already text. Still check encoding (UTF-8) and stray whitespace.

      
#️⃣ Markdown / HTML
Has useful structure (headings, lists). Strip markup and navigation but keep headings — they make good chunk boundaries and metadata.

🗑️
Garbage in, garbage retrieved
If extraction splits “an-
nual” into two tokens, merges two table columns, or repeats a page header in every chunk, the embeddings describe noise. Retrieval then misses the right passage — and the LLM never gets a chance to answer correctly.

  


  
  

    
5.2 Chunking

    
A chunk is a small passage of a document that is embedded and retrieved as one unit.

    

      
Why not embed a whole document?
One vector for a 40-page handbook blends leave, expenses, IT and security into a single “average meaning”. It matches everything a little and nothing well. And you could not fit many whole documents into the prompt.

      
What chunks give you
Focused vectors that match specific questions, small passages that fit in the prompt, and precise source citations (file, page, section).

    

    

      
SettingMeaningTrade-off

      
Chunk sizeMaximum length of a chunk (characters, words or tokens)Too small → fragments without enough context (“18 days” without “annual leave”). Too large → mixed topics, blurry vectors, more prompt tokens.

      
Chunk overlapHow much text consecutive chunks shareReduces the chance that a fact is cut in half at a boundary; costs duplicate storage and embedding work.

      
Fixed-sizeCut every N characters/tokensSimple and predictable; may split sentences and words.

      
Sentence-basedGroup whole sentences up to a size limitKeeps sentences intact; chunk lengths vary.

5.3 Embeddings

    
An embedding is a list of numbers (a vector) produced by a neural network to represent the meaning of a piece of text. Embedding models are trained so that texts with similar meaning end up with vectors that point in similar directions.

    

      
Why they help search
Keyword search needs shared words. “How many annual leave days are available?” and “Employees receive 18 days of annual paid leave.” share only some words, and a question like “How much holiday do I get?” shares none — yet the meaning matches. Embeddings capture that relationship.

      
Model-dependent
Different models produce different vectors, dimensions (e.g. 384 for all-MiniLM-L6-v2, 768 or more for others) and score ranges. Vectors from two different models cannot be compared. Always use the same model for documents and queries.

5.4 Vector search and similarity

    
Cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors: same direction → 1, perpendicular (unrelated) → 0, opposite → −1. Length does not matter, only direction. If vectors are normalised to length 1, cosine similarity is simply the dot product.

Similarity ≠ probability
A cosine of 0.82 does not mean “82% likely correct”. Scores are relative to a model, a dataset and a metric. One model may give related pairs 0.4–0.6, another 0.7–0.9. Compare scores within one setup; choose thresholds only after validating on real questions.

      
Why the top-1 chunk is not always enough or right
The answer may be spread over two chunks; a similar-looking chunk (old policy, different country) may outrank the right one; or no chunk may contain the answer at all. Top-K > 1 improves the chance the evidence is included, at the cost of more noise and tokens.

    

    
import numpy as np

a = np.array([0.9, 0.3])          # query (illustrative 2-D vector)
b = np.array([0.8, 0.5])          # document

cosine = a @ b / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))
print(round(float(cosine), 3))    # 0.974 -> almost the same direction

Step 3 — Generate embeddings


def load_model(name: str = MODEL_NAME) -> SentenceTransformer:
    try:
        return SentenceTransformer(name)
    except Exception as exc:  # network error, wrong name, corrupted cache ...
        sys.exit(
            f"Could not load embedding model '{name}': {exc}\n"
            "The first run downloads the model and needs internet access."
        )


def build_index(model: SentenceTransformer, chunks: list[dict]) -> np.ndarray:
    if not chunks:
        raise ValueError("The knowledge base is empty - add at least one chunk.")
    texts = [chunk["text"] for chunk in chunks]
    # normalize_embeddings=True -> every vector has length 1,
    # so a dot product equals cosine similarity.
    embeddings = model.encode(texts, normalize_embeddings=True, convert_to_numpy=True)
    return embeddings.astype(np.float32)  # shape: (num_chunks, 384)

  • sys.exit(...)Fails with a helpful message for network or name errors.

    •

  • normalize_embeddings=TrueScales every vector to length 1. Then dot product = cosine similarity, which keeps Step 4 simple and fast.

def retrieve(
    query: str,
    model: SentenceTransformer,
    doc_embeddings: np.ndarray,
    chunks: list[dict],
    top_k: int = 3,
) -> list[dict]:
    """Return the top_k most similar chunks with their scores and metadata."""
    if not query or not query.strip():
        raise ValueError("Query must be a non-empty string.")
    if top_k < 1:
        raise ValueError("top_k must be at least 1.")
    top_k = min(top_k, len(chunks))

    # Same model + same normalisation as indexing -> vectors are comparable.
    query_vec = model.encode([query], normalize_embeddings=True, convert_to_numpy=True)[0]

    # (num_chunks, 384) @ (384,) -> (num_chunks,) cosine similarities in [-1, 1]
    scores = doc_embeddings @ query_vec

    # Indices of the highest scores, best first.
    ranked = np.argsort(scores)[::-1][:top_k]

    results = []
    for rank, idx in enumerate(ranked, start=1):
        chunk = chunks[idx]
        results.append(
            {
                "rank": rank,
                "id": chunk["id"],
                "score": float(scores[idx]),
                "text": chunk["text"],
                "source": chunk["source"],
                "metadata": chunk["metadata"],
            }
        )
    return results

  
📐
The one line that does the search
scores = doc_embeddings @ query_vec multiplies a (6, 384) matrix by a (384,) vector → (6,): one cosine similarity per chunk. Because both sides are normalised, each value is in [−1, 1]. With a million chunks this brute-force approach gets slow, which is why vector databases use approximate nearest-neighbour indexes — a later topic.

"""Day 1 - RAG Fundamentals: first retrieval pipeline (no LLM, no API key).

Documents -> Embeddings -> Query encoding -> Cosine similarity -> Ranking -> Top-K

Run:
    python rag_retrieval.py
"""
from __future__ import annotations

import sys

import numpy as np

try:
    from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
except ImportError:  # clear message instead of a long traceback
    sys.exit("sentence-transformers is not installed. Run: pip install sentence-transformers numpy")

MODEL_NAME = "sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2"

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Step 2 - Knowledge base: small, pre-chunked company-policy passages
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
KNOWLEDGE_BASE: list[dict] = [
    {
        "id": "HR-001",
        "text": "Employees receive 18 days of annual paid leave per calendar year.",
        "source": "hr_leave_policy.pdf",
        "metadata": {"department": "HR", "updated": "2026-01-15"},
    },
    {
        "id": "HR-002",
        "text": "Remote work is permitted up to three days per week with manager approval.",
        "source": "remote_work_policy.md",
        "metadata": {"department": "HR", "updated": "2026-03-02"},
    },
    {
        "id": "FIN-001",
        "text": "Expense claims must be submitted within 30 days of the transaction date.",
        "source": "finance_expenses.pdf",
        "metadata": {"department": "Finance", "updated": "2025-11-20"},
    },
    {
        "id": "IT-001",
        "text": "The IT helpdesk operates from 9 AM to 6 PM on weekdays.",
        "source": "it_support_guide.txt",
        "metadata": {"department": "IT", "updated": "2026-02-10"},
    },
    {
        "id": "IT-002",
        "text": "Employees must change their network password every 90 days.",
        "source": "it_security_policy.pdf",
        "metadata": {"department": "IT", "updated": "2026-04-01"},
    },
    {
        "id": "FAC-001",
        "text": "Visitors must sign in at reception and wear a visitor badge at all times.",
        "source": "office_facilities.md",
        "metadata": {"department": "Facilities", "updated": "2025-09-05"},
    },
]


# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Step 3 - Load the embedding model and embed every chunk (indexing)
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
def load_model(name: str = MODEL_NAME) -> SentenceTransformer:
    try:
        return SentenceTransformer(name)
    except Exception as exc:  # network error, wrong name, corrupted cache ...
        sys.exit(
            f"Could not load embedding model '{name}': {exc}\n"
            "The first run downloads the model and needs internet access."
        )


def build_index(model: SentenceTransformer, chunks: list[dict]) -> np.ndarray:
    if not chunks:
        raise ValueError("The knowledge base is empty - add at least one chunk.")
    texts = [chunk["text"] for chunk in chunks]
    # normalize_embeddings=True -> every vector has length 1,
    # so a dot product equals cosine similarity.
    embeddings = model.encode(texts, normalize_embeddings=True, convert_to_numpy=True)
    return embeddings.astype(np.float32)  # shape: (num_chunks, 384)


# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Steps 4 and 5 - Similarity search wrapped in a reusable retrieve() function
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
def retrieve(
    query: str,
    model: SentenceTransformer,
    doc_embeddings: np.ndarray,
    chunks: list[dict],
    top_k: int = 3,
) -> list[dict]:
    """Return the top_k most similar chunks with their scores and metadata."""
    if not query or not query.strip():
        raise ValueError("Query must be a non-empty string.")
    if top_k < 1:
        raise ValueError("top_k must be at least 1.")
    top_k = min(top_k, len(chunks))

    # Same model + same normalisation as indexing -> vectors are comparable.
    query_vec = model.encode([query], normalize_embeddings=True, convert_to_numpy=True)[0]

    # (num_chunks, 384) @ (384,) -> (num_chunks,) cosine similarities in [-1, 1]
    scores = doc_embeddings @ query_vec

    # Indices of the highest scores, best first.
    ranked = np.argsort(scores)[::-1][:top_k]

    results = []
    for rank, idx in enumerate(ranked, start=1):
        chunk = chunks[idx]
        results.append(
            {
                "rank": rank,
                "id": chunk["id"],
                "score": float(scores[idx]),
                "text": chunk["text"],
                "source": chunk["source"],
                "metadata": chunk["metadata"],
            }
        )
    return results


# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Step 8 - Preview of the "augment" step: build the prompt an LLM would receive
# (no API call today - Day 2/3 sends this to an LLM)
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
PROMPT_TEMPLATE = """You are a company policy assistant.
Answer the question using ONLY the context below.
If the context does not contain the answer, reply exactly:
"Insufficient information in the available documents."
Cite the source of every fact in square brackets.

Context:
{context}

Question: {question}
Answer:"""


def build_prompt(query: str, results: list[dict]) -> str:
    context = "\n".join(f"[{r['source']}] {r['text']}" for r in results)
    return PROMPT_TEMPLATE.format(context=context, question=query)


def print_results(query: str, results: list[dict]) -> None:
    print(f"\nQuery: {query}")
    for r in results:
        print(f"  #{r['rank']}  {r['id']:<8} score={r['score']:.3f}  source={r['source']}")
        print(f"       {r['text']}")


# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Step 6 - Run example queries
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
if __name__ == "__main__":
    model = load_model()
    doc_embeddings = build_index(model, KNOWLEDGE_BASE)
    print(f"Indexed {len(KNOWLEDGE_BASE)} chunks -> embedding matrix shape {doc_embeddings.shape}")

    queries = [
        "How many annual leave days do employees receive?",
        "Can I work from home?",
        "What is the deadline for submitting expense claims?",
        "When can I contact IT support?",
        "What is the company's maternity leave policy?",  # NOT in the knowledge base
    ]
    for q in queries:
        print_results(q, retrieve(q, model, doc_embeddings, KNOWLEDGE_BASE, top_k=3))

    # Step 8 preview: what would be sent to an LLM for the first question
    first = retrieve(queries[0], model, doc_embeddings, KNOWLEDGE_BASE, top_k=2)
    print("\n--- Prompt preview (not sent anywhere today) ---")
    print(build_prompt(queries[0], first))

🎤 Consultant interview questions

  
1. Explain RAG to a client's CTO in two minutes. When would you not use it?
RAG keeps the LLM unchanged and, for each question, searches the client's documents for the most relevant passages, then asks the LLM to answer from those passages with citations. It suits large, private or frequently changing knowledge. I would not use it when the whole knowledge fits comfortably in the prompt and rarely changes (just prompt), when the need is a behaviour or format change (prompting or fine-tuning), or when answers require exact computation over structured data (query the database or call a tool instead).

  
2. Retrieval quality is poor. Where do you look first?
Upstream first: inspect extracted text and chunks for noise (headers, broken tables, hyphenation) and check chunk size/boundaries. Then confirm the same embedding model is used for documents and queries and that the index is up to date. Then look at the model choice for the domain and language, Top-K, metadata filters, hybrid keyword + vector search and re-ranking. Measure with a small labelled set of question → expected chunk pairs (e.g. hit rate @K) instead of eyeballing.

  
3. How do you choose chunk size and overlap?
Start from the document structure and the question types: FAQ entries or policy clauses can be single small chunks; long technical prose needs paragraph-sized chunks with some overlap. Respect the embedding model's token limit. Then evaluate a few configurations on real questions — chunking is an empirical choice with a trade-off between precision (small) and context completeness (large), and overlap trades duplication for fewer split facts.

  
4. Is a cosine score of 0.8 good? Can we use 0.7 as the "found an answer" threshold?
A score is only meaningful relative to the model, metric and corpus — it is not a probability. Some models compress scores into a narrow band. A threshold can help as one signal, but it must be calibrated on labelled examples (answerable vs not) and monitored. It should be combined with prompt instructions to abstain, re-ranking or relevance checks, and citation verification.

  
5. What changes when this prototype goes to production?
A proper ingestion job (loaders, cleaning, chunking, incremental updates and deletions), a vector database with ANN indexing and metadata filtering, access control so users only retrieve documents they may see, an LLM call with prompt templates, citations and abstention, evaluation of retrieval and answers, monitoring of latency/cost/quality, secrets in environment variables or a secret manager, and deployment as a FastAPI service in Docker — topics for the coming days.

Recap and Preparation for Day 2
⏱ 5 min


  

    
❓ The problem
LLMs know only their training data and the prompt. Private, changing documents need to be retrieved and put into the prompt.

    
🔀 Two pipelines
Ingestion (offline): extract → clean → chunk → embed → store. Query (online): embed question → search → Top-K → context → LLM → answer.

    
🧱 Building blocks
Chunks are retrievable units; embeddings represent meaning as vectors; vector search ranks chunks by cosine similarity.

    
🐍 What you built
A working semantic retrieval pipeline: real all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embeddings, NumPy cosine search, Top-K with scores and sources.

    
🔍 Retrieval ≠ generation
Retrieval finds evidence; it never writes an answer and can return related-but-wrong chunks. Generation needs an LLM, a prompt and an abstention rule.

    
🧭 Still missing
LLM call and prompt construction, citations, abstention, better ingestion and chunking, evaluation, a vector database and deployment.
